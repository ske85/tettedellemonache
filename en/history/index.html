Tette delle monache: history, origin and tradition

A dome of sponge cake so light it seems made of air, a heart of custard and a dusting of icing sugar that makes it look like a small snow-capped mountain. It is the most curious pastry of Altamura, a town in Puglia, southern Italy, and behind it lies a story of convents, closely guarded recipes and names that make people smile.

What tette delle monache are

Tette delle monache are small dome-shaped pastries made with a batter similar to sponge cake, but much airier: eggs whisked for a long time and very little flour, which rise in the oven until they are almost like a cloud. They are round, with a small peak on top.

Once cooled, they are filled with pastry cream or chantilly cream (pastry cream lightened with whipped cream) and finished with a generous layer of icing sugar. The result is a delicate contrast: dry and feather-light outside, soft and creamy inside.

In Puglia they are a classic of the Sunday guantiera, the tray of pastries brought to the table after family lunch, and they can be found in Altamura's pastry shops all year round.

The origin: the Poor Clare nuns of Altamura

Like many Italian sweets, this one was born within the walls of a convent. Tradition links tette delle monache to the Poor Clare nuns, the order of women founded by Saint Clare of Assisi, whose convents in Puglia and Abruzzo were for centuries true pastry workshops.

In Altamura the reference point is the Monastery of Saint Clare, in the heart of the old town. Here, according to the story handed down in the town, the cloistered nuns kept the original recipe for generations. Today the monastery is still a stop for anyone visiting Altamura to discover its flavours.

The exact dates and the name of whoever invented the pastry have been lost: as often happens with convent recipes, the story passed from hand to hand, from nun to nun, before reaching the pastry shops. This aura of legend is part of the pastry's charm.

Why they are called "tette delle monache"

The name, which literally means nuns' breasts, is meant to make people smile. The most obvious explanation is the shape: a soft dome with a peak on top that, put simply, looks like a breast. Linking it to nuns adds that touch of popular irony typical of many southern Italian sweets, where the sacred and the profane mix light-heartedly.

It is not an isolated case: in Sicily, for example, there are the minne of Saint Agatha and other convent sweets with equally daring names. In many areas, those who prefer a more discreet name simply call them sospiri ("sighs").

Pastry cousins: sise and sospiri

Tette delle monache have close relatives in other parts of Italy, probably born from the same convent tradition:

Sise delle monache

Typical of Guardiagrele, in Abruzzo: the same soft sponge and custard filling, but with three "domes" joined together.

Sospiri

Found across Puglia, for example in Bisceglie: a similar dough, but covered with a sugar glaze instead of icing sugar.

Tette delle monache

The Altamura version: a single dome with a peak, custard inside and icing sugar that makes it look snow-covered.

Other names

The same pastry changes name depending on the town, the pastry shop and who is telling the story. These are the most common names:

  • Tette delle monache or tette della monaca: the best-known name in Altamura and across Puglia.
  • Sospiri or sospiri pugliesi: the more discreet name, used by many pastry shops (sometimes for the glazed version).
  • Sise delle monache: the name used in Abruzzo, typical of Guardiagrele.
  • Seni delle monache, minne or zinne delle monache: regional and colloquial variations on the same wordplay.
  • Dolce delle monache di Altamura or dolce delle Clarisse: names that recall its convent origin.

Abroad, people looking for them often search for Italian nun pastry, Altamura cream pastry or sospiri pastry. Whatever the words, the pastry is always the same: the airy dome from Altamura, with custard inside and icing sugar on top.

Altamura, a town of bread and pastries

Altamura stands on the Murge plateau, about 45 kilometres from Bari, and is known worldwide above all for its PDO bread, the first bread in Europe to receive Protected Designation of Origin status. The town also boasts a splendid cathedral commissioned by Emperor Frederick II, an old town of narrow lanes and courtyards known as claustri, and the famous Altamura Man, one of the most important prehistoric finds in Europe.

In this land of ovens and wheat, tette delle monache are the sweet side of tradition: the most delicious souvenir to take home after a visit to the town.

How to enjoy and store them

  • Fresh is best: the sponge is at its finest in the first few hours, while it is still light and dry outside.
  • In the fridge: because they are filled with cream, they should be kept cool and eaten within a day or two.
  • Icing sugar last: if possible, dust it on just before serving, so it stays white and does not melt.
  • With coffee: at breakfast or after lunch, they are perfect with an espresso, or an iced coffee in summer.

Frequently asked questions

What are tette delle monache?

They are a traditional pastry from Altamura, in Puglia (southern Italy): a feather-light dome of sponge cake, filled with pastry cream or chantilly cream and dusted with icing sugar.

Why are they called tette delle monache?

The name, which literally means nuns' breasts, refers to the rounded shape with a small peak on top, and to the link with convents, especially the Poor Clare nuns, who according to tradition created the recipe and kept it for generations.

Where do they come from?

Apulian tradition links them to Altamura and to the Monastery of Saint Clare in the old town. A very similar pastry, sise delle monache, is typical of Guardiagrele in Abruzzo.

How should they be stored?

Because they are filled with cream, they should be kept in the fridge and eaten fresh, ideally within a day or two. The icing sugar is best dusted on just before serving.

What other names are they known by?

They are also called tette della monaca, sospiri or sospiri pugliesi, sise delle monache (in Abruzzo), seni delle monache or, colloquially, minne and zinne delle monache.

What is the difference from sospiri?

They are closely related: sospiri have a similar dough but are covered with a sugar glaze, while tette delle monache are dusted with icing sugar.
